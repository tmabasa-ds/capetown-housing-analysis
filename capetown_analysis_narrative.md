# Cape Town's Disappearing Rentals: Mapping the Airbnb Effect

**By Tiyani Mabasa**  
*Data: Inside Airbnb (September 2025) | City of Cape Town Open Data Portal*

---

## Why I looked into this

I've been watching Cape Town change for a while now. Not in the obvious ways (the new developments, the coffee shops, the murals), but in the quieter, harder-to-see ways. The family that's been in the same flat in Sea Point for fifteen years suddenly can't renew their lease. The domestic worker who used to commute forty minutes now commutes two hours because she got pushed further and further out. The young professional who grew up in Woodstock and can't afford to live anywhere near where she grew up.

These aren't isolated stories. They're a pattern. And I wanted to see if the data backed it up.

So I pulled the Inside Airbnb dataset for Cape Town: 26,877 listings scraped in September 2025, mapped against the City of Cape Town's official suburb boundaries. What I found wasn't surprising, but seeing it laid out spatially makes it harder to look away.

---

## The scale of the problem

Let's start with the basics. Cape Town has **26,877 Airbnb listings**. That's not a footnote. That's a significant portion of the city's rental housing stock operating as short-term accommodation.

Of those listings:

- **82.6% are entire homes or apartments.** Not a spare room, not a couch. A whole unit that could be someone's home, listed for nightly rental.
- The **median price is R1,526 per night**, which works out to roughly R45,780 a month.
- The **Western Cape median monthly salary is approximately R8,500**.

That last comparison is the one that should stop you cold. The median Airbnb listing in Cape Town costs more per month than five times what the median resident earns. These properties aren't priced for locals. They're priced for international visitors and, increasingly, digital nomads: remote workers from Europe and North America who earn in dollars and euros and find Cape Town extraordinarily affordable by their standards, even at R30,000 a month.

The problem is that R30,000 a month is not affordable for the people who actually live here.

---

## Where the pressure is concentrated

Not all of Cape Town is feeling this equally. When you map the data, a clear geographic pattern emerges.

The **Atlantic Seaboard and City Bowl** (Sea Point, Green Point, Camps Bay, Clifton, the City Centre) are carrying the heaviest load. These are the suburbs where Airbnb density is highest, entire-home listings dominate, and nightly prices push into the tens of thousands.

The **southern suburbs** (Constantia, Tokai, Noordhoek) show high prices but slightly lower listing volumes, reflecting their character as larger estate-style properties rather than apartment blocks.

The **eastern suburbs, Cape Flats, Khayelitsha, Mitchells Plain** barely register on the map at all. Almost no Airbnb presence. Which tells its own story about which parts of the city are considered desirable by the short-term rental market, and which aren't.

The displacement isn't random. It follows the exact geography of desirability, and that geography has a racial and economic history in this city that goes back a very long way.

## A closer look at Clifton and the Atlantic Seaboard

If you want to understand the ceiling of this market, look at Clifton.

**99.5% of Clifton's Airbnb listings are entire homes.** Not 80%. Not 90%. Essentially every single listing is a complete property withdrawn from the permanent housing market. The median nightly price is R6,666, which works out to R200,000 a month.

Bantry Bay sits at 87.2% entire homes and a R5,215 median nightly rate. Fresnaye at 86.6% and R5,994. Llandudno at 92.7% and R7,053, the highest median nightly price in the dataset.

These are not numbers that local residents can compete with. A landlord in Clifton isn't choosing between renting to a local family at R15,000 a month and leaving the unit empty. They're choosing between that family and a digital nomad couple paying R200,000 a month on Airbnb. The economics of that decision are not complicated.

---

## Woodstock: the sharpest edge of gentrification

Woodstock deserves its own conversation because it represents something different to the Atlantic Seaboard suburbs. Camps Bay was always expensive. Clifton was always exclusive. Woodstock wasn't.

Woodstock is (or was) a working-class, predominantly coloured neighbourhood sitting just east of the City Bowl. It has a long history as a mixed-income community, with families who have lived there for generations alongside a creative and industrial character that made it interesting to developers and young professionals in the early 2010s.

That gentrification process started before Airbnb. But the platform accelerated it dramatically.

In the current dataset, Woodstock has **412 Airbnb listings**, of which **84% are entire homes**. The median nightly price is **R931**, which sounds modest compared to Camps Bay, but that works out to **R27,930 a month**. In a suburb where families were historically paying R4,000 to R6,000 a month in long-term rent, the Airbnb economy has completely rewritten the pricing logic of the area.

What makes Woodstock particularly significant is who gets displaced. Atlantic Seaboard residents being priced out can, in many cases, find alternatives in the southern suburbs or further out. Woodstock residents being displaced often have fewer options, and the communities that made Woodstock what it was are being scattered across the Cape Flats.

There's a particular cruelty in the fact that Woodstock's cultural appeal (its murals, its food markets, its character) was built by the very communities now being pushed out of it. The neighbourhood became desirable because of them. And that desirability is now pricing them out.

**84% of Woodstock's Airbnb listings are entire homes. The monthly equivalent of the median listing is R27,930. The median local salary is R8,500.**

Those three numbers tell a complete story.

## The digital nomad question

It would be unfair to frame this purely as a digital nomad problem. Short-term rental platforms were displacing housing before remote work became mainstream, and the majority of Airbnb demand in Cape Town still comes from conventional tourists.

But the growth of remote work has added a new and qualitatively different pressure. A tourist stays for a week. A digital nomad stays for three months, or six, or a year, renewing accommodation on Airbnb because it's cheaper and more flexible than a formal lease. They're not tourists. They're quasi-residents, occupying long-term housing stock at short-term rental prices.

South Africa introduced a Digital Nomad Visa in 2024, broadly welcomed as an economic development measure. The projected income from attracting remote workers is real. But the cost of that income isn't being distributed equally. It's being extracted from local housing markets, and the people paying the price are the ones who can least afford to.

I'm not arguing that digital nomads are villains. Most of them are just people working remotely and looking for a pleasant place to live. But the system that accommodates them (short-term rental platforms operating at scale with minimal regulation) is creating consequences for local communities that haven't been seriously reckoned with.

## What the map shows that the numbers can't

When you look at the pressure map (the one showing entire homes removed from the market by suburb), what strikes you isn't just the hotspots. It's the emptiness.

The eastern half of the city barely registers. The Cape Flats, home to a significant portion of Cape Town's population, is almost entirely absent from the Airbnb economy. Not because people there don't need housing, but because the platform doesn't serve them. It serves the parts of the city that visitors want to be in.

And that spatial divide (the intense red pressure on the Atlantic Seaboard, the grey emptiness of Khayelitsha and Mitchells Plain) mirrors a divide that has defined Cape Town for decades. The Airbnb economy didn't create that divide. But it is deepening it, suburb by suburb, listing by listing.

---

## What could be done

I'm a data analyst, not a policy maker. But the data suggests a few things worth considering.

**Registration and caps.** Several cities (Amsterdam, Barcelona, New York) have introduced hard caps on short-term rental listings per host, or requirements that hosts register their properties. Cape Town has informal guidelines but no hard enforcement mechanism.

**Entire-home restrictions.** The distinction between renting a spare room and removing an entire home from the housing market is significant. Platforms could be required to distinguish between these, with different rules applying to each.

**Levy redistribution.** If short-term rental income is going to be extracted from neighbourhoods like Woodstock and Sea Point, some mechanism for returning value to those communities (through affordable housing funds, for example) would at least partially address the imbalance.

None of these are simple to implement. All of them involve tradeoffs. But the current situation (essentially unregulated short-term rental at scale in a city with a severe housing shortage) also involves tradeoffs. We're just making them invisibly, and the people paying the price aren't the ones making the decisions.

## Data and methodology

All Airbnb listing data is sourced from [Inside Airbnb](http://insideairbnb.com/cape-town), a non-commercial project that scrapes and publishes Airbnb listing data for public interest research. The dataset used here was scraped in September 2025 and contains 26,877 listings.

Suburb boundary data is sourced from the [City of Cape Town Open Data Portal](https://odp-cctegis.opendata.arcgis.com) (Official Planning Suburbs dataset).

Listings were spatially joined to suburbs using GeoPandas. Of 26,877 listings, 26,815 (99.8%) were successfully assigned to a suburb. The remaining 62 likely fall just outside official suburb boundaries.

Salary benchmarks are drawn from StatsSA Western Cape wage data. The R8,500 median monthly figure is a conservative estimate for the broader working population and includes both formal and informal sector workers.

The "housing pressure score" used for the choropleth map is calculated as: total listings x (entire home percentage / 100). It represents the estimated number of entire homes removed from the long-term rental market in each suburb.

All code and data used in this analysis is available in the project repository.
